Welcome back to this edition, where we tackle a question that frequently arises in my conversations with team members and mentorship sessions: the significant and often challenging evolution through a career path that progresses from an individual contributor (IC) to a first-line manager, then to a manager of managers - second line manager, and finally, to an executive role.

This path is far more than a simple climb; it demands profound shifts in perspective, focus, and the very nature of the aptitudes required to succeed. Over the past years at Microsoft and Salesforce, I've had the immense privilege of mentoring and coaching numerous aspiring and current managers. This process, which I hope has significantly enriched them, has undeniably been a deeply enriching experience for me as well.

Aspiring managers often seek to understand the characteristics of executive leadership or the nuances of managing other managers, while existing managers ponder the complexities of vertical career growth. Let's unpack these transitions, exploring the core differences and essential learnings at each critical juncture.

### The Foundational Leap: From Individual Contributor to First-Line Manager

The initial and perhaps most visible transition is the one from being an individual contributor to stepping into a first-line management role. As an Individual Contributor (IC), the primary focus is typically centered on the "I" – the individual person. The goal is to perform one's job to the best of one's ability, to achieve personal results, and in some ways, to think about oneself. While it's true that individual contributors can and often do demonstrate important leadership by supporting junior colleagues and helping others in the organization, the fundamental focus remains on individual performance and contribution.

However, the moment you are given the opportunity to become a first-line manager, the viewpoint must undergo a radical change. The core and fundamental shift is from thinking about "I" to thinking about "WE". In all my mentorship sessions and discussions, I consistently emphasize that becoming a leader means shifting your attention to the group. This requires abandoning a degree of self-centeredness and moving beyond the need to continuously demonstrate your own skills, validation, or focus on yourself.

This marks a significant psychological step back. You must realize that your capabilities as an individual, which led to your promotion, have already been tested and recognized by the organization. They no longer need to be continuously proven in the same way. What the organization now demands is for managers to concentrate on how to empower and valorize their people. The manager's success is no longer measured solely by their individual output but must be a direct reflection of the success of their team, the organization, and the individuals within it.

This essential shift requires the person to learn not to be at the center of attention. The manager must become somewhat "hidden," not always in the front row, not seeking continuous visibility or striving to be the "superstar". Instead, the role involves giving voice to the people. A truly effective manager, in my view, must cultivate a strong focus on being, in essence, the PR manager for their team members. This means having the capacity and the focus to highlight and showcase their people, to facilitate and manage connections for them, and to enable their rapid growth.

Thus, the manager transforms from being an individual contributor to becoming an enabler of others' success. This principle of enabling success is absolutely fundamental. While it's true that individual contributors should also help others succeed – because success should never be purely individual but a team achievement – for managers, this principle is applied exponentially. It becomes fundamental. A manager's constant work is dedicated to ensuring their people succeed, because they understand that if their people succeed, the manager will, by reflection, succeed. This is a fundamental paradigm change when moving from being an individual contributor to a manager.

Think of it like an orchestra: you might have been an excellent violinist, perhaps even showing leadership within the orchestra as an individual. But then you become the conductor. The conductor's job is to coordinate all the instruments to ensure they work well together, to highlight individual instruments, and to bring out the characteristics of the single musician for the collective sound. This makes the orchestra conductor a fitting metaphor for the role of a manager. Another apt metaphor is a basketball coach. The coach might have played basketball in the past but wasn't necessarily the best player. Their importance to the group lies in their ability to assemble top players, make them play at their best, ensure they play in a coordinated manner, and work for the success of the team. They help the players understand that individual effort generates limited or no value if played alone.

A crucial concept to internalize is that being a good individual contributor is not the same as being a good manager. It is unfortunately common in organizations to see excellent individual contributors promoted to management positions simply because they were top performers. This can be a profound error, or at least potentially problematic, if promotion to management relies solely on a person's individual successes. We often see this with account executives promoted to sales management roles just because they were great AEs, and the same applies in other domains. Individual achievement should not be the sole criterion for identifying future managers. Being a manager is a fundamentally different role requiring different attitudes like humility, empathy, and a strong focus on People.

If, as a manager, you continue to act as the "top player," you are likely to fail or at least not provide the organization with your maximum potential as a managerial figure. More often than not, this creates significant problems within the organization and for the people working with you. Therefore, organizations should focus on other methods, such as incentives or alternative forms of recognition, to reward top individual performance, rather than automatically promoting top players to coaching roles. Promoting an IC solely because they are a top performer and believing they "deserve recognition" this way is a big mistake for managers who do the promoting.

### The Very Critical Step: From First Line Manager to Second Line Manager

Moving from being a first-line manager to a second-line manager – the person who manages other managers – is, in my opinion, the most critical step of all. It is even more critical than the initial transition from individual contributor to first-line manager. This heightened criticality stems from several reasons.

Firstly, you are now managing other managers, not individual contributors. As an FLM, you have a direct, operational, and guiding role with your team members, allowing you to have a direct capacity to create positive or negative impact. As a second-line manager (SLM), your action and impact on the individuals lower in the hierarchy are indirect. They are decoupled from or a consequence of your actions concerning the first-line managers you oversee. This indirect influence necessitates the ability to build the right level of trust and confidence with the managers in your organization. When you communicate a message to your managers, or when a manager of managers passes down messages, there must be the confidence and security that the message will subsequently be passed down correctly to the individual people.

This underscores the paramount importance of clarity in communication. Because messages are filtered through multiple levels, it is crucial that they are clear and consistently understood among the managerial figures. This ensures the message lands on individuals in the field as homogeneously as possible, preventing disparity caused by different managers interpreting or transferring messages differently. Thus, trust between the SLM and their first-line managers, and clarity in communication, are the first two fundamental elements that become increasingly important.

Beyond the trust from the SLM to their direct managers, the ability to create trust and collaboration among the managers themselves is critically important. I hold a strong belief in fostering an open relationship built on trust. Trust is the fundamental element that allows organizations to excel. It is that essential element that enables organizations to overcome most problems. Trust becomes fundamental within a managerial group. With my leadership teams, I have always worked significantly on fostering a strong trusting relationship not only between myself and the individual managers but also among the managers themselves.

During our leadership team meetings, I often encourage individual managers to discuss openly, to give feedback – even negative feedback – to their peers without fear of judgment or seeming unlikeable. The sole objective should be growth as a managerial group, as a group of leaders. While we certainly ask for openness and constructive feedback from individual contributors too, this becomes even more critical and indispensable among managers. It's often taken for granted that managers, being focused on objectives, should naturally possess this attitude of trust and openness to feedback. However, this often isn't the case, because managers are also just people, influenced by biases, past experiences, and potentially their own previous managers. Therefore, a significant task for a manager of managers is to actively work on their managerial community to create shared learning, collaboration, trust, and confidence among the team members.

The second major challenge in becoming a manager of managers is that the distance from the field, the distance from the individual people, significantly increases. For someone like myself, who is characteristically very empathetic and values connection, intimacy, and trust with people, I have found it difficult that as you grow within the organization and more managerial layers are added, this distance increases. It becomes impossible to know every single individual in the organization with the same level of detail, intimacy, trust, and dialogue that you had with your team members as a first-line manager. This reality can be frustrating and complex. For me, accepting this was very complex.

Furthermore, it's difficult to accept that as you move up the hierarchy, people may be less willing to speak with you directly. This decrease in willingness to interact is not usually personal. It's often because people feel a sense of hierarchy, perhaps hesitate to bypass their direct first-line manager, or simply don't know the higher-level person as well as their immediate manager. There are inherent difficulties. This reality of reduced direct interaction with individuals is something that happens to all second-line managers and those at high organizational levels. It is an important condition you must accept, understand, digest, and learn to manage appropriately. This might involve proactive steps like scheduling planned monthly or quarterly discussions with employees in your organization.

These are the major changes when moving from FLM to SLM:

  * The need to build a significant level of trust with your managers, as they are the ones who manage the organization and execution.
  * The resulting necessity for the ability to delegate effectively and accept that you will lose maximum control over activities. Control decreases as you ascend the hierarchy, making the level of trust placed in delegation fundamental.
  * Accepting the increased distance from people.
  * Accepting that people may find it difficult to speak with you due to your higher level in the organization, which leads to changes in psychological dynamics and engagement.



These are the fundamental aspects to consider when a first-line manager aims to develop a vertical career into second-line management.

### Reaching the Apex: From Management Levels to Executive

The transition from managerial levels to executive roles amplifies the challenges experienced by second-line managers. Difficulties with clarity, communication, and engaging people become three or four times more significant depending on the specific executive level. An executive carries all these difficulties from the SLM role, only amplified. 

However, there is another fundamental aspect that becomes even more stressed and amplified at the executive level, although it is also present at managerial levels: the significant shift in focus from a high interest and focus on the well-being of individual people to being a key organizational stakeholder deeply focused on bringing benefits and achieving company objectives. While care for people remains fundamental, the executive must fundamentally prioritize the company and its goals above all else. This shift and strong focus on being an important stakeholder within the management team, and thus needing to put the company and its objectives first, is something that can be particularly disruptive and difficult for very empathetic individuals. It can cause significant upheaval and challenges for them. Last but not least, there is another element that increases for executives which is the decision scale impact. This is sometimes to take into consideration and it’s an aspect that will lead to higher preparation and intense brain work and fewer decisions during the year.

In summary, the journey from individual contributor to executive leadership requires successive, complex transitions, each demanding a fundamental change in mindset. The path moves from focusing primarily on personal contribution to enabling the success of an entire team, then to leading effectively through other managers, and ultimately, to championing organizational objectives above all else while managing amplified communication and engagement challenges. Successfully navigating these levels hinges on building strong trust – both with your direct reports (managers) and among them – ensuring crystal clear communication, adapting to increasing distance from day-to-day operations and individual interactions, and developing the capacity to delegate effectively. Recognizing these profound shifts and embracing the evolving demands are absolutely crucial for continuous growth and effectiveness at each stage of the leadership journey.
