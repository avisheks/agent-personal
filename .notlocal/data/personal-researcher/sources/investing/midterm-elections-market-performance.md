## Presidential Election Cycle Theory

Origin: Yale Hirsch, Stock Trader's Almanac (data back to 1833). Pattern:

| Year of Term | Description | Average S&P 500 Return (1960-2024) |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Post-Election | +8.6% |
| Year 2 | Midterm | -0.1% (WEAKEST) |
| Year 3 | Pre-Election | +12.3% (STRONGEST) |
| Year 4 | Election | +7.3% |

From Stock Trader's Almanac (2020 edition, longer historical period):
- Post-election year: +3%
- Midterm year: +4%
- Pre-election year: +10.2%
- Election year: +6%

## Pre-Midterm Performance Data

Average S&P 500 return in midterm election years (Year 2, 1960-2024): -0.1%
- 9 out of 16 midterm years (56%) produced negative returns
- Median midterm year return: -0.3%

U.S. Bank research (31 midterms, 125 years): 12 months leading into midterms averaged +2.9% vs long-term average of 8.9%.

Worst midterm years:
- 1974: -30.2% (oil embargo, Watergate)
- 2002: -21.1% (dot-com bust, 9/11 aftermath)
- 2022: -14.5% (aggressive Fed rate hikes)
- 1966: -12.8% (inflation concerns, Vietnam escalation)
- 1962: -9.3% (Cuban Missile Crisis)

## Post-Midterm Rally Data

- Average S&P 500 return in post-midterm years: +12.3% (1960-2024)
- 14 out of 16 post-midterm years were positive (88%)
- S&P 500 averaged +6.5% in first 3 months after midterm Election Day
- Positive 12-month returns followed EVERY midterm since 1950

Strongest post-midterm rallies:
- 1995: +32.1% (after 1994's -3.8%)
- 1975: +22.2% (after 1974's -30.2%)
- 2019: +21.8% (after 2018's -8.0%)
- 2003: +20.6% (after 2002's -21.1%)
- 2023: +18.3% (after 2022's -14.5%)

## Nasdaq Specific Data

- Nasdaq average midterm year return: approximately -0.5% (MarketWatch)
- Nasdaq average post-midterm year return: approximately +30%
- Since 1971 (Nasdaq start): Year 3 averaged +28.8%
- Higher beta = more extreme swings both directions

Worst Nasdaq midterm years: 2022 (-33.1%), 2002 (-31.5%)
Best Nasdaq post-midterm years: 1999 (+85.6%), 2003 (+50.0%), 2023 (+43.4%)

## Counter-Examples

Post-midterm rally failures:
- 1987 (post-1986): -8.9% (Black Monday crash)
- 2011 (post-2010): -3.1% (European debt crisis, U.S. credit downgrade)

Midterm years that were NOT weak:
- 1982: +18.8% (Volcker rate cuts)
- 1986: +19.4% (bull market)
- 1998: +23.5% (dot-com bubble)
- 2014: +12.7% (QE bull market)
- 2006: +10.8% (housing bubble peak)
- 2010: +10.5% (QE2 recovery)

## Explanations for Pattern

1. Front-loaded pain: Presidents implement unpopular policies early in term
2. Uncertainty resolution: Once congressional control decided, policy direction clearer
3. Divided government: Gridlock after midterms historically favors markets
4. Stimulus ramp-up: Incumbents boost economy before re-election
5. Mean reversion: Beaten-down valuations create buying opportunities

## Statistical Caveat

U.S. Bank research: formal statistical testing found the pattern is NOT statistically significant given high variability and small sample sizes. In 11 of 31 midterm years, external factors overwhelmed any election effect.
