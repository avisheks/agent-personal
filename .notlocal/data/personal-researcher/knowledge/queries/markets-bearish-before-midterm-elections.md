## Short Answer

**Yes, historically markets have been bearish or flat before midterm elections**, but with important caveats:

- S&P 500 midterm year average (1960-2024): **-0.1%** (vs +8.9% long-term)
- Nasdaq midterm year average: **-0.5%**
- 56% of midterm years produced negative S&P 500 returns
- 12 months leading into midterms averaged only +2.9% (U.S. Bank, 31 midterms)

**However**: The pattern is NOT statistically significant (U.S. Bank formal testing). 44% of midterm years were positive, some strongly so (1998: +23.5%, 1986: +19.4%, 1982: +18.8%).

## The Full Presidential Cycle

| Year of Term | Avg S&P 500 Return | Hit Rate (Positive) |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (Post-Election) | +8.6% | ~69% |
| **Year 2 (Midterm)** | **-0.1%** | **44%** |
| Year 3 (Pre-Election) | +12.3% | 88% |
| Year 4 (Election) | +7.3% | ~75% |

Source: Calculated from S&P 500 data, 1960-2024. Theory originated by Yale Hirsch (*Stock Trader's Almanac*). ^[[Presidential Election Cycle Theory]]

## The Post-Midterm Rally (More Actionable)

The stronger signal is what happens AFTER midterms:
- Average Year 3 return: **+12.3%**
- 14 of 16 post-midterm years positive (88%)
- Every midterm since 1950 followed by positive 12-month returns
- Nasdaq Year 3 average since 1971: **+28.8%**

Largest swings (from midterm weakness to post-midterm strength):
- 1974→1975: -30.2% → +22.2% (52pp swing)
- 2002→2003: -21.1% → +20.6% (42pp swing)
- 2022→2023: -14.5% → +18.3% (33pp swing)

^[[Post-Midterm Rally]]

## Why the Pattern Exists

1. Presidents front-load unpopular policies in Years 1-2
2. Policy uncertainty peaks before midterm election day
3. After midterms: divided government → gridlock → markets approve
4. Incumbents stimulate economy in Years 3-4 for re-election

^[[Midterm Election Market Weakness]]

## When It Breaks

The pattern failed in:
- **Strong midterm years**: 1982, 1986, 1998, 2006, 2010, 2014 (all positive)
- **Weak post-midterm years**: 1987 (Black Monday), 2011 (EU crisis)
- **External shocks overwhelm cycle**: In 11 of 31 midterms, macro factors dominated

## Caveat

U.S. Bank's statistical analysis: "the 'midterms cause weak returns' conclusion **doesn't hold up reliably**" under formal testing. Wide variability and small sample size prevent confident predictions. Economic fundamentals remain the primary driver.
