# Presidential Election Cycle Theory

The **Presidential Election Cycle Theory** was proposed by Yale Hirsch in the *Stock Trader's Almanac* with data going back to 1833. It posits that U.S. stock markets follow a predictable 4-year pattern tied to the presidential term, with distinct performance characteristics for each year of the cycle. ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

## The Four-Year Pattern

The theory identifies a consistent cycle where stock market performance varies systematically based on the year within a presidential term:

| Year of Term | Description | Average S&P 500 Return (1960-2024) |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | Post-Election | +8.6% |
| Year 2 | Midterm | **-0.1%** (weakest) |
| Year 3 | Pre-Election | **+12.3%** (strongest) |
| Year 4 | Election | +7.3% |

The pattern shows Year 2 (midterm years) as the weakest performing period, while Year 3 (pre-election years) delivers the strongest returns. This creates a pronounced trough-to-peak cycle within each presidential term. ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

## Historical Performance Data

### Midterm Year Weakness

Since 1960, midterm election years have shown consistently weak performance:
- 9 of 16 midterm years (56%) produced negative S&P 500 returns
- Median midterm year return: -0.3%
- 12 months leading into midterms averaged +2.9% (vs 8.9% long-term average)

The worst midterm years include 1974 (-30.2%), 2002 (-21.1%), 2022 (-14.5%), 1966 (-12.8%), and 1962 (-9.3%). ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

### Post-Midterm Rally

The theory's most reliable component is the post-midterm recovery pattern:
- 14 of 16 post-midterm years (88%) were positive since 1960
- Average 12-month return after midterms: +12.4%
- Positive 12-month returns followed EVERY midterm election since 1950
- S&P 500 averaged +6.5% in first 3 months after Election Day

Notable post-midterm rallies include 1995 (+32.1%), 1975 (+22.2%), 2019 (+21.8%), 2003 (+20.6%), and 2023 (+18.3%). ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

## Nasdaq Amplification Effect

The Nasdaq shows the same directional pattern but with more extreme volatility due to its higher beta characteristics:
- Midterm year average: approximately -0.5%
- Post-midterm (Year 3) average since 1971: +28.8%
- Worst Nasdaq midterm years: 2022 (-33.1%), 2002 (-31.5%)
- Best post-midterm years: 1999 (+85.6%), 2003 (+50.0%), 2023 (+43.4%)

^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

## Theoretical Explanations

The cycle is attributed to political incentives and policy timing:

### Front-Loading Strategy
Presidents implement unpopular but necessary policies (spending cuts, tax increases) early in their terms when political capital is highest, creating economic headwinds in Years 1-2. ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

### Pre-Election Stimulus
Incumbents typically stimulate the economy in Years 3-4 through favorable policies to improve re-election prospects, boosting market performance. ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

### Uncertainty Resolution
Once midterm elections determine congressional control, policy direction becomes clearer, reducing market uncertainty and enabling the [[Post-Midterm Rally]]. ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

## Counter-Examples and Limitations

### Pattern Failures
Notable exceptions include:
- **Strong midterm years**: 1982 (+18.8%), 1986 (+19.4%), 1998 (+23.5%), 2014 (+12.7%)
- **Weak post-midterm years**: 1987 (-8.9% due to Black Monday), 2011 (-3.1% due to European debt crisis)

^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]

### Statistical Significance
U.S. Bank's formal statistical testing found the pattern is **NOT statistically significant** given high variability and small sample sizes. In 11 of 31 midterm years studied, external economic factors (oil crises, financial bubbles, monetary policy) overwhelmed any election-related effects. The pattern is directionally correct but cannot reliably predict future returns. ^[midterm-elections-market-performance.md]
